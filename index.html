The data

303 patient records, 13 clinical signals.

This study trains and compares five machine-learning models on the widely used Cleveland heart-disease dataset — clinical measurements such as age, blood pressure, cholesterol, chest pain type, and exercise response, paired with a diagnosis of heart disease. It's the same dataset and methodology used in the underlying research notebook, run again here so every number on this page is live and real.

The source project's title references viral infection as a research motivation, but the dataset itself contains only standard clinical and diagnostic features — no viral-infection variables are present. Everything below is scoped honestly to what the data actually supports: cardiovascular disease prediction from clinical features.

A pattern emerges

Patients with a higher-risk diagnosis skew toward lower peak heart rates and older ages, while lower-risk patients cluster toward higher achieved heart rates — visible as the two colors separating diagonally across the plot. It's a tendency in this sample, not a rule for any one patient, and correlation here doesn't establish that either variable causes the other.

Performance

How well did each model do?

All five models trained on an identical 80/20 split, same random seed, same standardized features. Naive Bayes and SVM edge out the rest on this dataset; the simple, fully interpretable Logistic Regression is close behind and powers the live demo above.

Closing

So, what did we learn?

01

Multiple factors matter

No single measurement drives the model's estimate. Chest pain type, exercise-induced angina, ST depression, vessel count, and heart rate all move the needle together — risk here is a combination, not a single symptom.

02

Some signals carry more weight

ST depression during exercise (oldpeak), peak heart rate, and the number of major vessels colored by fluoroscopy consistently ranked as the strongest predictors across the trained Random Forest — see the ranking above.

03

Prediction can be personalized

The same model that scores 85%+ accuracy on held-out patients can take any individual's clinical profile and return a specific, explainable estimate — as shown in the simulator above.
